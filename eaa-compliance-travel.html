EAA compliance for travel — what the sector needs to know

In a 2025 survey of large Spanish companies, 40% of travel websites were graded 'not valid' on technical adequacy. Travel is in EAA scope. Here is what compliance requires and what enforcement looks like.

In the Barómetro de Accesibilidad Web 2025, 40% of the travel websites audited were graded ‘not valid’ on technical adequacy. A user who cannot complete a booking search, compare options, or reach the checkout does not appear in your analytics as a lost conversion. They simply do not appear at all. The survey covered the websites of 204 large Spanish companies.

Travel represents independence. A disabled user who cannot complete a booking online cannot plan or travel independently. The EAA was designed specifically to remove that dependency. Under the Act, travel booking platforms, accommodation sites, and transport services with digital customer journeys are in scope. The enforcement window is open.

What EAA covers in the travel sector

The EAA applies to the digital journey, not the physical one. Physical airport accessibility, in-flight service, and ground handling are not in EAA scope. The following digital touchpoints are:

  • Online booking engines: flights, accommodation, car hire, tours, packages
  • Mobile applications used for booking and trip management
  • Seat selection and add-on interfaces
  • Payment flows and checkout
  • Online and mobile check-in interfaces
  • Customer account and booking management portals
  • E-tickets, boarding passes, and travel confirmation documents (digital)

Each stage of a booking journey is a potential accessibility barrier and each is in EAA scope. More touchpoints means more failure points. A platform that passes a homepage audit may still block users at seat selection, checkout, or mobile check-in.

What the data shows

The Barómetro de Accesibilidad Web 2025 audited the websites of 204 large Spanish companies. Its travel results:

  • 40% were graded ‘not valid’: they failed the survey’s technical adequacy criteria.
  • 20% were graded ‘not compliant’ on the legal requirements the survey checked.

The distinction between ‘not valid’ and ‘not compliant’ matters. ‘Not valid’ means the site fails WCAG technical adequacy criteria. That is a technical condition. ‘Not compliant’ means the site fails the legal standard the EAA requires. An organisation can be technically partial and legally non-compliant simultaneously.

Why travel is especially exposed

Travel booking flows are among the most complex digital journeys in any sector. Flight search, accommodation comparison, seat selection, loyalty integration, add-ons, and payment each represent a distinct interaction point. Each must be accessible. Organisations that have addressed one stage often have significant barriers at another.

The revenue case is equally concrete. A user who cannot complete a booking does not leave a trace. They do not appear as an abandoned basket or a failed checkout. They simply never enter the funnel.

Enforcement context

Ireland: The CCPC enforces the EAA for digital travel services. Ireland attaches criminal liability under the EAA: fines up to €60,000 and imprisonment up to 18 months on indictment.

Netherlands: the ACM enforces across e-commerce and digital services, including travel platforms. Its review of approximately 100 major webshops found that in 61% of cases, ordering was entirely impossible for users with assistive technology. Fines can reach 10% of annual turnover and no ceiling is verified. Financial penalties have been legally available since June 2025. None has been issued. The ACM’s published posture is compliance first.

Sweden: PTS has launched 28 supervisory investigations covering e-commerce and digital services broadly. Travel platforms with Swedish consumer exposure are in scope. Sanktionsavgift fines range from SEK 10,000 to SEK 10,000,000.

France: the civil society enforcement model confirmed in the Carrefour compliance order applies equally to travel. Disability organisations have the same legal standing to bring proceedings against travel platforms as they do against retailers. A second case, Auchan, was dismissed on a procedural threshold specific to legal entity structure, despite the court finding the site inaccessible; it is currently under appeal before the Court of Appeal of Douai.

We have found no EAA enforcement decision against a named travel platform in the seven markets we monitor. The enforcement framework is in place and active across all markets.

In June 2026, the EU also reached agreement on new air passenger rights guaranteeing adjacent seating for disabled passengers at no extra cost. Accessibility obligations in the travel sector are expanding, not contracting.

What EAA compliance requires

EAA compliance for travel platforms comes down to four areas of work. Microenterprises providing services are exempt (Article 4(5)).

Technical conformance against the requirements in Annex I of the Act, usually assessed against EN 301 549 V3.2.1 (WCAG 2.1 Level AA). Version 4.1.1, published in September 2026, carries WCAG 2.2, but no version is yet cited under the EAA. Targeting WCAG 2.2 now is sensible preparation.

Published accessibility statement: information explaining how your services meet the accessibility requirements, usually called an accessibility statement. Saying what is not yet accessible, the standard you work to, and how users can report barriers is good practice.

Active governance: a named owner for accessibility, a documented testing process, and a remediation workflow. Compliance is not a one-off audit. It is a continuous process.

Documentary evidence: records of testing, decisions, and remediation activity. In any enforcement contact, what you can demonstrate matters as much as what you have done.
