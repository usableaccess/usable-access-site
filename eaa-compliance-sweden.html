EAA compliance in Sweden — PTS enforcement

PTS did not wait for complaints. It launched systematic inspections of e-commerce operators on 15 October 2025, named H&M, IKEA and Coop among its first investigations, and has two separate penalty mechanisms available. The enforcement risk and the accessibility work are two separate tracks.

Sweden’s implementation of the European Accessibility Act is enforced by PTS (Post- och telestyrelsen), the Swedish Post and Telecom Authority. PTS has taken a proactive approach to enforcement, actively inspecting organisations rather than waiting for complaints to be filed. Our detailed account of how enforcement is actually running in Sweden covers the inspection programme, the organisations under investigation, and what PTS can require.

On 15 October 2025, PTS launched a programme of systematic e-commerce inspections, examining the accessibility of online retailers. It opened seventeen cases in 2025 and eleven more on 3 March 2026, bringing the total to 28. It is a running programme rather than a single wave, and PTS says which companies come next is not yet decided. Among the organisations named are H&M, IKEA, Coop, ICA, and Systembolaget, spanning fashion, furniture, food retail, supermarkets, and specialist retail. Every one of them is a retailer, which is why e-commerce is the sector carrying the most enforcement attention across Europe. The programme remains at the investigation stage: PTS has not issued a penalty against any organisation on the list, and no administrative fine has been confirmed under any European Accessibility Act transposition in the seven markets we monitor.

Banking services are now under supervision too

On 8 September 2026, PTS announced it had begun the first supervision of banking services under the Act. The supervision is directed at larger actors established in Sweden, will run through 2026 and 2027, and PTS has said it will publish the names of the banks as new cases are opened.

The first stage does not begin with PTS testing anything. It begins with what the companies themselves reported about their deficiencies. PTS says it may then go on to check whether the websites and mobile applications are designed in a way that makes them perceivable, operable, understandable and robust.

Sweden’s law names no technical standard

Lag (2023:254) does not mention EN 301 549. Neither does Förordning (2023:676). Neither mentions WCAG.

What section 2 of the Act refers to instead is “a harmonised standard under Article 2(1)(c) of Regulation (EU) No 1025/2012”, the generic definition by cross-reference. No standard is named, because none has been cited for this law.

PTS describes what it assesses against directly: whether services are designed so that they are perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. Those are the four functional principles from the directive itself, not criteria from a standard. PTS’s own standards page describes the work of harmonising a standard against this law as still under way.

Conformance with WCAG 2.2 AA remains the most practical way to build an accessible service. In Sweden it is not what the law asks you to demonstrate.

Five authorities, not one

Förordning (2023:676) splits supervision across five bodies. 28 § gives PTS electronic communications, banking services and e-commerce services. 29 § gives Mediemyndigheten audiovisual media access services. 30 § gives Myndigheten för tillgängliga medier e-books. 31 § gives Konsumentverket and Transportstyrelsen elements of passenger transport.

If you sell online to Swedish consumers, your supervisor is PTS.

What PTS can do

PTS has broad enforcement powers under Lag (2023:254). The ladder runs uppmaning → föreläggande, with or without vite → sanktionsavgift. It can investigate any organisation offering digital products or services to consumers in Sweden, issue remediation notices with binding deadlines, impose financial penalties, and prohibit the sale of non-compliant products and services within Sweden.

That last power, the market ban, is significant. For many organisations, being prohibited from selling into the Swedish market is a more severe consequence than any financial penalty. PTS can apply it independently of or alongside financial sanctions.

Sweden’s two penalty types

We have read the Act and the Ordinance directly. The description of PTS’s enforcement powers below is PTS’s own account of what it can do, citing section 25 of the Act and section 24 of the Ordinance. We have not read section 25 in its own words.

Sweden operates two distinct financial penalties under its EAA enforcement framework, and the relationship between them matters. Under section 38 of Lag (2023:254), a sanktionsavgift cannot be imposed where the same breach is already covered by a föreläggande om vite. They are alternatives for a given violation, not cumulative.

Fine type 1

Sanktionsavgift

SEK 10,000 – SEK 10,000,000

Set by statute at “lägst 10 000 kronor och högst 10 000 000 kronor” (Lag 2023:254, section 39). Applied for failing to report or cooperate with PTS. This penalty applies regardless of whether the underlying accessibility issues have been fixed. An organisation that has completed its remediation work but failed to respond to a PTS request can still face a Sanktionsavgift. The enforcement risk and the accessibility work are two separate tracks. Both need managing.

Fine type 2

Vite

Variable

PTS may issue a föreläggande with or without vite (section 35). Applied where accessibility issues remain unremediated after a deadline set by PTS. Where PTS has issued a remediation notice with a deadline, failure to meet that deadline triggers a Vite. The amount is set by PTS and tied to the specific failure. Unlike the Sanktionsavgift, the Vite is directly linked to the state of the accessibility work.

The maximum Sanktionsavgift is SEK 10,000,000. The figure that matters legally is the krona amount. No penalty decision has been published, and the first wave of supervisory investigations has not yet concluded. We will update this page when that changes.

Why Sweden enforces this way

Sweden has a strong culture of disability rights and public accessibility expectations. Consumer complaints about inaccessible digital services tend to arrive quickly. PTS’s proactive inspection approach reflects the expectation that compliance obligations are known and acted on, not discovered through enforcement.

Organisations that have not yet engaged with EAA requirements should not assume that the absence of a complaint means no exposure. PTS is selecting organisations for inspection independently. Receiving a supervisory notice without prior warning is the experience of the companies named in the first wave.

The mandatory reporting obligation

Like the Netherlands, Sweden requires organisations to engage with PTS on their compliance position. Failure to respond to a PTS request or to participate in a supervisory investigation triggers the Sanktionsavgift. This obligation is separate from and in addition to the accessibility work itself.

Organisations operating in Sweden need two things in place: an active accessibility programme, and a process for responding to PTS contact promptly. Neither alone is sufficient.

Companies are reporting their own failures

The Act obliges economic operators to report accessibility deficiencies to the supervising authority. In its 2025 annual report, PTS records 322 such reports received from economic operators, alongside 114 complaints from the public. The 114 and the 124 above are counts taken at different dates, not a contradiction.

That number matters more than it first appears. When PTS announced its supervision of banking services in September 2026, it said the first stage would not begin with PTS testing anything. It would begin with what the companies had reported about their own deficiencies.

Source: PTS Årsredovisning 2025, report number PTS-ER-2026:1.

The microenterprise exemption, and it is written in euros

Section 2 defines a microenterprise as a company with fewer than ten employees and an annual turnover or annual balance sheet total not exceeding two million euro. Not kronor. The Act uses the directive’s currency without converting it.

Section 10 is unusually direct about what follows. Services provided by microenterprises “behöver inte uppfylla tillgänglighetskraven”: they need not meet the accessibility requirements. That is a full exemption for services, not merely relief from documenting an assessment. The products side of the same section gets only the documentation relief.

SFS 2026:1026 amends section 2, the definitions paragraph, with effect from 20 November 2026. The microenterprise threshold is unchanged. Anything quoting Swedish definitions after that date should be re-read against the amended section.

What Swedish enforcement means in practice

The companies named in the first 28 investigations were not selected because complaints were filed. PTS opened supervision on its own initiative. No penalty has been imposed on any of them.

Alongside its own-initiative inspections, PTS has received 124 public complaints so far under the EAA: 110 concerning digital services, primarily e-retail websites, and 14 concerning products including ATMs and ticketing machines. Nineteen companies, about 20%, received more than one. Product complaints are mostly about cash machines. The volume confirms that Swedish consumers are aware of their EAA rights and willing to exercise them. Own-initiative inspections and public complaints are two separate enforcement routes running simultaneously.

The key question for Sweden: if PTS tested your Swedish-facing digital product or service today, what would it find? The answer to that question determines your enforcement exposure, as does whether you have a documented process to address it.

Penalty decisions from the first wave of investigations are expected throughout 2026. When those decisions are published with named organisations and fine amounts, they will clarify the enforcement posture in practice. Organisations that have not yet assessed their position should do so before those decisions arrive.

Supervision figures last checked 13 September 2026. The 28 e-commerce cases and the banking supervision are both live and will change.

Acting now rather than responding later

The pattern in Sweden, as in the Netherlands and Germany, is that enforcement moves faster than most organisations expect. PTS launched systematic inspections within months of the EAA taking effect. The organisations that started compliance work early are building a governance record. The organisations that have not yet started are not.

Acting now means setting your compliance position on your own timeline. Acting after a PTS notice means responding to theirs.
